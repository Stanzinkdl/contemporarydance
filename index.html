    01 · The idea

    Every technique is the same body, asking different questions.

    Graham, Limón, Cunningham, Release, Contact, Gaga. Each one trains the same bones and muscles. What changes is what they ask of them.

    Where is your weight? Does the spine stay tall or curve? Do you keep your balance or give it away? Change those answers and you change the dance.

    02 · Weight

    Up and away, or down and in.

    The first number is where the weight lives. Ballet and Cunningham pull up out of the floor. Butoh goes the other way: knees bent, weight dropped, the body sinking toward the ground.

    The figure is Butoh's numbers with only this one changing.

    03 · Torso

    A column, or a curve.

    Martha Graham made the spine the subject. Her contraction hollows the torso from the pelvis. Her spiral twists it around its own axis.

    Set the torso to zero and Graham's body stands like a column. Turn it up and the contraction comes back.

    04 · Dynamics

    A long breath, or a drum.

    The same movement can be poured slowly or struck sharply. Watch the hand's trail. At low values it draws smooth arcs. At high values it snaps from place to place and holds.

    Katherine Dunham's rhythmic, isolated hips and ribs live near the percussive end.

    05 · Initiation

    Hands first, or hips first.

    William Forsythe's improvisation tools start movement from imagined points and lines around the body. The hands draw, and the body follows.

    Move initiation to the centre and the same figure starts from the pelvis. The arms go quiet and only follow.

    06 · Balance

    Keep it, or give it away.

    Doris Humphrey called dance "the arc between two deaths": standing still, and lying flat. José Limón built a technique in that arc. You fall, you catch yourself, you rebound.

    At zero the body holds its balance. At the top it tips, suspends, and recovers.

    07 · Floor

    The floor as a partner.

    In older techniques the floor is mostly where class starts. In David Zambrano's Flying Low it is where the dance happens: you spiral in and out of the ground at speed.

    08 · Structure

    A vocabulary, or a set of instructions.

    Some techniques are set exercises you repeat until they become yours. Others give you images and tasks and leave the shape to you.

    Gaga, Ohad Naharin's practice, has no mirrors and no fixed steps. As the number drops, the regular rhythm breaks up and wanders.

    09 · Contact

    One body becomes two.

    The last number is the other person. In 1972 Steve Paxton made a form whose whole subject is a moving point of touch: Contact Improvisation. Two dancers lean, give and take weight.

    Eight numbers, then. Next: what happens when we put every technique into the same space?

    10 · The parameters

    Eight questions, in depth.

    The eight numbers are not arbitrary. Each one borrows from an established way of looking at movement: Rudolf Laban's Effort and space theory, Irmgard Bartenieff's body connectivities, and the principles the founders of these techniques wrote and taught themselves. For each parameter below you'll find what it measures, where the idea comes from, how to see it in a class, the scoring guide used to place each technique, and where all twelve techniques fall.

    Scores run from 0 to 1. They are informed judgements against the scoring guide, not laboratory measurements. Tap any technique to read why it got its number.

    11 · The space

    Twelve techniques in one space.

    Each technique is a point with eight coordinates. Eight dimensions can't be drawn, so they are squeezed into three (principal component analysis). Those three keep of the differences between techniques. Points that sit close together ask the body similar things. The grey arrows show which way each original number pulls.

    Drag to rotate · scroll to zoom · tap a technique

    Most of the cube is empty. Like Raup's shells, the techniques people have actually developed fill only a small part of what is possible.

    12 · What tells them apart

    Which numbers matter most.

    Not every parameter separates techniques equally. If all twelve agree on something, it can't tell them apart. The spread (standard deviation, σ) shows how much each parameter varies across techniques. Principal component analysis then finds the combined directions along which techniques differ most. These are the axes of the morphospace above.

    13 · Kinship

    Who resembles whom.

    Similarity is 1 minus the distance between two techniques' eight numbers, scaled to a 0–100 range. The rows are ordered by the first principal component, so related techniques sit next to each other. Tap a square to compare a pair on every parameter, and read why they differ most.

    14 · Lineage

    Who learned from whom.

    Similarity and ancestry don't always match. Cunningham danced for Graham and then rejected most of what she taught. Gaga comes from a Graham-trained dancer, yet in the space it sits closer to Forsythe and Countertechnique than to Graham.

        16 · Recorded dancers

        The same skeleton, moved by a real dancer.

        Everything above is choreographed from each technique's class material. These clips are different: they are motion-capture recordings of two dancers improvising modern-dance material, played back on the same anatomical skeleton. Only the recorded joint rotations are used, so the bones keep their real proportions. The recordings aren't labelled by technique, but they show what real weight shifts, turns and arm paths look like. Try them at quarter speed.
